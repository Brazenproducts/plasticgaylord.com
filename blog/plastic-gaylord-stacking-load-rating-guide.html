Plastic Gaylord Stacking and Dynamic Load Ratings Explained

Plasticgaylord Editorial • October 2026

Load rating is the single most misread number on a plastic gaylord spec sheet. Buyers see a bold figure printed next to the word capacity and assume it describes every condition the container will face in their operation. It does not. A gaylord carries different safe loads depending on whether it is sitting still, moving on a forklift, or stacked three high in a trailer. Understanding the distinction between static, dynamic, and stacking ratings is what separates a container that performs from one that fails in transit.

Static Load Rating

Static load is the weight a gaylord can hold when it is stationary and fully supported from below, such as resting on a pallet on the warehouse floor. This is almost always the highest number on the spec sheet because the container is not fighting inertia or point loads. Static rating is useful for storage planning but should never be used to size dynamic handling. Treating a static number as a handling limit is the most common sourcing mistake and the fastest route to a blown-out wall or floor.

Dynamic Load Rating

Dynamic load describes the safe weight while the container is in motion, being lifted, carried, or moved by a forklift or conveyor. Acceleration, braking, and uneven lift points all introduce forces beyond the raw weight of the contents, so the dynamic rating is meaningfully lower than the static figure. Sourcing teams should size their fill weight against the dynamic rating, not the static one, because handling is where containers actually fail. Reusable plastic gaylord containers from Elipacko publish both figures so engineers can design to the correct limit rather than guessing.

Stacking Load and the Compression Factor

Stacking load is the weight a gaylord can bear on top of it from other loaded containers above. This is governed by the container wall and corner-post compression strength and is heavily affected by time, humidity, and temperature. A rating valid for a quick cross-dock may not hold for a container sitting loaded in a hot warehouse for weeks. Always ask whether a stacking figure is instantaneous or sustained, and derate for your actual dwell time and climate. In cold storage the numbers shift again, which is why cold-chain operations require ratings validated at temperature.

Reading Capacity Numbers Correctly

A complete spec should give you static, dynamic, and stacking ratings as three separate numbers, plus the test conditions behind each. If a vendor supplies only one figure, treat it as marketing until proven otherwise. The safe working load for your operation is the lowest relevant rating for the toughest condition the container will see, divided by an appropriate safety factor. For most industrial and food lanes, a safety factor between 1.5 and 2.0 against the governing dynamic or stacking number is prudent.

Matching Containers to the System

Load ratings do not exist in isolation. A gaylord rated for heavy stacking is only as strong as the pallet beneath it and the dividers that distribute point loads inside. Pairing correctly rated containers with compatible pallets and inserts keeps the whole unit load within spec. The Takeaway

Never buy a gaylord on a single capacity number. Demand static, dynamic, and stacking ratings with their test conditions, size your fill against the governing dynamic or stacking figure, and apply a safety factor for your climate and dwell time. Done correctly, load rating analysis turns container selection from a guess into an engineering decision.
